Nine reasons behind one silence

A system that watches you reason about a decision can see that you have said almost nothing about money. It cannot see whether the money is already settled or too painful to look at. In our study, nine distinct reasons sat behind what the system recorded as one identical pattern.

The setup

We ran 62 people through a reflection conversation about a real decision they were facing, across 13 different decision topics. The agent tracked how much ground each person had covered in three registers: the analytical, the emotional, and the lived. At 232 points across those conversations, the agent paused, told the person what it had noticed, and asked two questions before doing anything about it. First, does this match your sense of it? Second, why is it like that?

That second question is the one nobody usually asks a system to ask. Most adaptive agents observe a gap and act on it. We observed the gap and handed it back.

What came back

The answers sorted into nine categories, and they are not variations on a theme. They are genuinely different states of the world:

  • Deliberate prioritisation. I know that side exists. I decided it matters less.
  • Self-diagnosed focus. I notice I do this, and I do not fully endorse it.
  • Emotional driver. Something I feel is pulling me there.
  • Decision nature. The decision itself does not have much of that side to it.
  • Thinking style. This is just how I think about everything.
  • Interaction attribution. You did not ask me about it.
  • No accessible reason. I do not know why.
  • Pattern disputed. Your reading of me is wrong.
  • Content-level response. Answering the topic rather than the question about the topic.

Eight of these arose from a single observable state. The same numbers, on the same dashboard, at the same point in a conversation, meaning eight different things. If you are building a system that adapts to what it observes, that is the whole problem in one sentence.

The part I did not expect

Given the choice of where to take the conversation next, people moved toward the side they had underexplored in 74.4% of cases. They diverged from what the agent would have chosen on its own 54.7% of the time. Both things at once. People do want to go where the light is thin, and they do not want to be routed there.

The comfortable prediction was that handing over control would make the conversation lazier, because people would avoid the hard side. It did not. It made the route different and the destination roughly the same.

Why I keep coming back to this

There is a strong pull in this field toward systems that decide on your behalf, gently, for your own good. I understand the appeal and I do not think it is malicious. But the interpretive gap does not go away with a better model. A larger model still sees the pattern and not the reason. Making the observation visible costs one sentence and gives the person the one thing they actually have that the system does not.

The full paper is Why This and Not That?, in Findings of EMNLP 2026. The numbers above are from Sections 5.1 and 5.2. Everything the study measures is about the process of deciding. It does not measure whether anyone's decision turned out well, and I would not claim that it does.
